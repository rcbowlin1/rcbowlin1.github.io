It was a long day at the screens today — the kind where you look up and it's dark — and my head keeps drifting to Arkansas.

This summer my wife and son and I flew back to visit my family — my parents and my sister — and spent two days down in Hot Springs on Lake Catherine. Time on the boat, swimming, fishing, the kind of big loud family dinners you don't get enough of, and, inevitably, a sunburn I earned honestly. A month later I went back, this time for a much more serious occasion: my fantasy football draft, on Lake Ouachita (say it "WASH-uh-taw" and locals will let you live). Both trips were the good kind of tired. On days like today I just want to go back.

So I figured I'd write down a few things about Hot Springs that most people don't know — because it turns out this little spa town has one of the strangest résumés in America.

**Start with the hot springs themselves.** They're real, and they're old — the water bubbling up here fell as rain something like four thousand years ago. People have believed for centuries that it heals (the science is more "warm bath, good vibes" than medicine, but let's not ruin it), and the federal government thought enough of it to set the land aside in **1832** — four decades before Yellowstone. That makes it one of the oldest protected pieces of ground in the country. By the Gilded Age it had become "the American Spa," and the grand bathhouses of Bathhouse Row were where you went to "take the waters" in serious style.

**It's a national park — with a brewery in it.** Superior Bathhouse Brewery sits in one of those old bathhouses on the Row, and it holds a genuinely singular distinction: it's the only brewery inside a U.S. national park, and the only brewery *anywhere* that makes its beer with thermal spring water — the same 143-degree water that made the town famous, cooled and turned into a pint. I can confirm it works.

**It's the birthplace of baseball's spring training.** In 1886, Cap Anson brought his Chicago White Stockings — now the Cubs — down to train in the warm air and soak in the baths, and the annual ritual of spring training was basically invented on the spot. For decades the game's biggest names came through; a young Babe Ruth trained here and reportedly hit a home run so long it's still argued about locally. Eventually the teams chased better facilities in Florida and Arizona, but it started in Arkansas.

**And then there's the part they don't put on the brochure.** For most of the 20th century, Hot Springs ran wide-open illegal gambling — casinos hiding in plain sight behind cigar-store fronts — protected by a machine-politics arrangement that reached all the way up to the governor's office. That made it a favorite hideaway for the biggest names in organized crime. Al Capone kept a regular suite at the Arlington Hotel. Lucky Luciano was living here when the law finally caught up and arrested him in 1936. And Owney Madden, a genuine New York gangster, essentially *retired* to Hot Springs and ran the town's rackets from a comfortable distance.

Here's the twist I got backwards until I read up on it: the story isn't that the mob wanted to turn Hot Springs into Vegas. It's the other way around. **Hot Springs was America's Sin City before Las Vegas was anything at all** — and Bugsy Siegel, who built the Vegas Strip, is said to have taken more than a little inspiration from what already worked here. The casinos finally got shut down in the late 1960s, and the crown passed to the desert. Vegas is the sequel.

The one form of gambling that stayed legal — and is still going strong — is the ponies. Oaklawn has been running thoroughbred races here since 1905, the respectable cousin who somehow kept the whole family's reputation intact.

So that's Hot Springs: healing water older than the pyramids' neighbors, the accidental birthplace of spring training, a beer brewed from a national park, and a mob town that was Vegas before Vegas. Not bad for a place I mostly go to sit on a boat and get sunburned with people I love.

After a day like today, that sounds about perfect. I'll be back soon.
